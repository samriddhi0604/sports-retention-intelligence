01 THE SET-UP

Real matches.
Real movies.
Simulated viewers.

There's no public dataset of who watches what on a streaming platform, or who cancels — that's the platform's most guarded commercial secret. So this project uses a different strategy: take every piece of real data that is public, and simulate only the one thing that genuinely can't be found anywhere.

What's simulated, and why that's still honest: 5,000 subscribers were generated, because no real per-user viewing history exists to use instead. But every one of their simulated viewing sessions picks an actual movie from the real catalog above, and is timed against the real match calendar — so the behavior is invented, but everything it's measured against is not.
02 FINDING ONE

The sports effect is real

Split the simulated subscribers into two groups — the ones who stayed, and the ones who cancelled — and look at how much of their viewing happened on a real match day.

The two groups don't just differ a little. Subscribers who stayed subscribed did roughly twice as much of their watching around real IPL matches as subscribers who left.

46.5%
23.0%
Stayed subscribed · 3,257 people Cancelled · 1,722 people
In plain terms: if sports-watching genuinely had nothing to do with staying subscribed, seeing a gap this size purely by chance would be astronomically unlikely — the actual odds work out to about 1 in 10²³². That's not a typo; there are more zeroes in that number than there are atoms worth counting. This is the project's headline result, and everything below acts on it.

04 FINDING THREE

Who's about to leave?

A simple formula — a logistic regression — was trained to weigh three habits against each other and estimate a subscriber's risk of cancelling.

The formula essentially asks: does this person watch sports, does their taste range across genres, and do they generally enjoy what they pick? Each answer pulls their risk score up or down. Here's how strongly each one actually mattered, once the model was trained on real behavior patterns:

05 FINDING FOUR

Winning them back

Knowing who's at risk is only useful if you know what to recommend them. Three approaches were tried here, in increasing sophistication — and the honest result of each is reported as-is, including the ones that didn't pan out.

01

Gateway content

Do subscribers who later became power-users have something in common in what they watched before that ramp-up? If so, that's a "gateway" worth recommending to others.

Sports was NOT the gateway
02

Collaborative filtering

Factorize who-watched-what into a map of viewing taste, then check whether titles with nothing in common on paper still cluster together in that map — a sign of real hidden affinity.

Inconclusive — flagged as unproven, not oversold
03

Hybrid, cold-start-aware model

Blend that taste-map with straightforward genre/rating similarity, leaning more on genre for subscribers too new to have a reliable taste profile yet.

Works as designed; a validation gap disclosed openly

A far simpler rule was tested alongside all of this, as a baseline: just recommend sports content to anyone at-risk whose sports engagement sits below the average retained subscriber. 98.9% of at-risk subscribers qualified — which sounds impressive, but is really just a restatement of Finding Three's strongest coefficient, not a new discovery.

06 POST-MATCH REVIEW

What this isn't

A clear-eyed limitations list is a feature of this project, not something to bury in a footnote.

Simulated population

Real per-user streaming data doesn't exist publicly, anywhere. Every subscriber here is simulated — but grounded in the real match calendar and real film catalog throughout.

Daily, not hourly

Match times aren't published in the source data, and the public traffic API only reports daily totals — so every measurement here works at day-level precision, not hour-level.

A genuine null result

The gateway-content experiment did not find sports content to be a meaningful entry point in this data. Reported honestly rather than reframed into a win.

An unproven signal, flagged as such

The collaborative-filtering "hidden affinity" result didn't hold up under scrutiny — it's more consistent with a statistical artifact than a genuine discovery, and is reported that way.
